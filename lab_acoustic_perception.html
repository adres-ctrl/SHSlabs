Key ideas
Phase only matters when you compare waves
  • One full cycle is 360°. Two waves of the same frequency keep a stable phase relationship.
  • In phase (0°): peaks line up, the amplitudes add, and the sum is larger (constructive interference).
  • 180° out of phase with equal amplitude: every peak meets a trough, and the sum is zero (destructive interference).
  • Unlock the frequencies and set them a few Hz apart. The phase relationship keeps drifting, so the sum swells and fades. These are called beats, and the beat rate equals the difference between the two frequencies.
Part 2
Noise-Canceling Headphones (ANC)
A noise-canceling headphone samples the incoming noise with a microphone and plays an "anti-noise" signal. This simulation contains a 100 Hz engine hum, a 250 Hz cabin rumble, and a 2000 Hz voice-range sound. The ANC circuit sends the noise through a band-pass filter (50 Hz to 1000 Hz) before making anti-noise. Only sounds inside that band get cancelled. Sounds outside the band pass straight through. The circuit also needs a small amount of time to process the sound, which is the processing delay.

Key ideas
Why ANC handles hum better than voices
  • ANC is destructive interference. The anti-noise has to be the same frequency and amplitude, and 180° out of phase.
  • The band-pass filter decides which sounds get anti-noise. It combines a high-pass filter (removes below 50 Hz) and a low-pass filter (removes above 1000 Hz). Anything outside the band reaches your ear unchanged.
  • A processing delay becomes a phase error. The same delay is a much bigger part of a short (high-frequency) cycle than a long (low-frequency) cycle.
  • Steady, low-frequency noise (engines, HVAC) is easiest to cancel. Higher-frequency and sudden sounds mostly get through, and only the passive seal of the ear cups reduces them.
Part 3
The Doppler Effect
A moving sound source passes a listener. The rings are wavefronts spreading out at the speed of sound (343 m/s). Watch them bunch up in front of the moving source and spread out behind it. The effect is easier to see at higher speeds. The source itself never changes frequency. Only the frequency the listener receives changes.

Key ideas
Approaching is higher, receding is lower
  • Approaching: the source catches up to its own waves. Wavelengths in front get shorter, so the frequency heard is higher.
  • Receding: the source moves away from the waves behind it. Wavelengths get longer, so the frequency heard is lower.
  • The "approaching" and "receding" readouts assume the source is far away and heading straight toward or away from you: f × 343 / (343 − v) and f × 343 / (343 + v).
  • Speed is set in meters per second. 1 m/s is about 2.24 mph.
